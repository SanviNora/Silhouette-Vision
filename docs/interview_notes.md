# Silhouette Vision — Interview Notes

A running log of the decisions we make, why we made them, and the questions an interviewer is
likely to ask about each. It's updated as the project is built; once the build is finished it
becomes the basis for mock-interview practice.

Data-specific Q&A lives in [data_guide.md](data_guide.md#interview-cheat-sheet).

## Likely questions (answers to be filled in with our own results)

**Models**
- *Tell me about a bug you found.* GR-Lite's published code leaves out DINOv3's rotary position embeddings (RoPE). The weights still load perfectly, because RoPE has no learned parameters, so nothing errors. I suspected it, ran both versions on LookBench, and the RoPE version won by 4 points at Recall@1 and 11.5 points at Recall@10. Lesson: **'it loads and runs' is not 'it's correct'; validate on a benchmark.**
- *What is RoPE?* Rotary position embedding: instead of adding a position vector, it rotates query and key vectors by an angle that depends on each patch's position, so attention scores depend on relative position. Without it, the transformer treats the image patches as an unordered set, so the layout of the garment is lost.
- *200 queries — is that enough?* It's a quick screen, with a margin of error of roughly ±7 points on Recall@1, so the 1-point GR-Lite vs Marqo gap at R@1 is noise. The gaps at R@5/R@10 are larger and consistent. The full benchmark (1,000+ queries per subset plus 58k distractors) is the real test.
- *What's the difference between a CLIP/SigLIP-style model and a DINO-style model?*
  CLIP/SigLIP learn from image–caption pairs, so images and text share one space (text search, zero-shot labels). DINO learns from images alone with self-supervision, and is often better at fine visual detail but can't read text.
- *Why two models instead of one?* → fill in with the LookBench results.
- *How did you choose between them?* → LookBench Recall@1, speed (images/s on an M4), and whether text search is needed.
- *SigLIP vs CLIP?* SigLIP uses a sigmoid loss on each image–text pair instead of a softmax over the whole batch. It trains well at smaller batch sizes and is usually stronger at the same size.

**Engineering**
- *Your evaluation crashed the machine. What happened?* It decoded all 69k benchmark images into RAM before embedding (tens of GB on a 16 GB Mac). Fix 1: stream compressed bytes and decode per batch. Fix 2: macOS DataLoader workers each got a pickled copy of the 2 GB of bytes, so I switched to decode threads in one process, which was also 4× faster. Fix 3: checkpoint every 4,096 images after a battery death nearly lost an overnight run.
- *How did you make embedding 229k images practical on a laptop?* 512 px thumbnails made once (Myntra originals are up to 1800×2400), resumable chunked embedding, a single long-lived DataLoader, and a small model. Also: the first attempt ran at 4–24 img/s because the Mac was swapping 18.8 GB. **Always check memory and swap before blaming the model.**
- *Why not just use the most accurate model?* Accuracy per unit of cost: GR-Lite is ~3 Recall@1 points better on LookBench but ~8× slower and image-only. For a CPU-hosted demo that also needs text search, the smaller model wins, unless our own benchmark shows a bigger gap.
- *Why is cosine similarity a dot product in your index?* The embeddings are L2-normalized, so the dot product equals cosine similarity.
- *Exact vs approximate nearest-neighbour search?* At about 230k vectors an exact search (one matrix-vector product) is fast enough, so we don't use an index at all. Approximate methods (HNSW, IVF-PQ in FAISS or a vector database) pay off at millions of vectors, trading a little recall for speed and memory.
- *Why no FAISS?* It crashed next to PyTorch on macOS (two OpenMP runtimes in one process). Rather than use an "unsafe" workaround, I measured and found we didn't need it. **Measure before adding infrastructure.**
- *How do filters work without hurting results?* They're applied before ranking (scores of excluded items set to −∞), so "bags only" still returns k bags. Filtering *after* a top-k search can return too few results.

**Evaluation**
- *Your number didn't match the paper. What did you do?* Checked in order: data (gallery sizes matched exactly), preprocessing (Marqo's transform already matches the paper's), then metric definition. The paper's "fine Recall@1" counts category + main attribute, not the exact product. Recomputed from cached embeddings in seconds: 63.8% vs 62.8% published. **Reproduce the baseline before claiming improvements.**
- *How did you avoid overfitting to the benchmark?* Every tuned setting (blend weight, colour weight, trick parameters) was chosen on even-indexed queries and reported on odd-indexed ones. Also, no gallery labels were used for re-ranking: LookBench labels all 58k distractors "noise data", so a label-based category filter would have trivially inflated the score.
- *Why did query expansion / database augmentation not help?* They average a vector with its neighbours, which helps when many relevant items exist (e.g. landmark retrieval). Here each query has 1–4 true matches among ~60k near-duplicate distractors, so neighbours are mostly wrong items.
- *Why does blending two models beat both?* Their errors are weakly correlated: GR-Lite (image-only, DINOv3) is better on real photos, Marqo (image–text, SigLIP) on AI-generated ones. Averaging cosine scores keeps each one's confident correct matches. Cost: two models at query time.
- *How much does fashion-specific training matter?* Generic CLIP ViT-B/16: 20.5% exact R@1; Marqo: 51.6% (2.5×); on street photos 11.8% → 41.8% (3.5×).
- *Precision@10 with lift vs chance, why?* Raw precision is misleading when classes are imbalanced (Fit is 66% "Regular Fit" by chance). Lift = precision / chance shows what the embedding actually knows: brand 105×, article type 15.5×, colour 4.2×.
- *What did your first tests show?* 11 real photos: logo/pattern and silhouette are captured very strongly (8/8 Louis Vuitton monogram bags for an LV query; 8/8 wide-leg jeans). Weaknesses: subtle colour (burgundy→red), busy runway shots, multi-item photos. Similarity scores fall from ~0.83 to ~0.72 on the hard cases, so they work as a confidence signal.
- *How would you fix the weaknesses?* Colour: text refinement (partial fix, tested) or colour-aware re-ranking. Multi-item: detect and crop each garment, then search per item; a category filter already fixes it manually (tested).
- *Why does the model get the logo but miss the colour?* Contrastive training on product captions rewards brand/pattern/shape words that appear in titles; colour is often a single word and less emphasized. Lighting and backgrounds (a burgundy shoe on a patterned sofa) also shift perceived colour.
- *How do you know the search is good?* → LookBench Recall@K against the public leaderboard; precision@K on Myntra labels.
- *What is Recall@1 here?* The share of query photos whose top result is the exact same product.

**Deployment and licensing**
- *Why doesn't your public demo show the Farfetch catalog directly?* The images were scraped and have no license. Hosting them publicly would be redistributing copyrighted photos. The public app uses properly licensed galleries (Myntra MIT, Visuelle CC BY-NC-SA with credit), shows Farfetch as analysis, and loads Farfetch images from Farfetch's own URLs rather than copying them.
- *What does CC BY-NC-SA require?* Credit the authors (BY), non-commercial use only (NC), and share derivatives under the same license (SA).

**Product understanding (Phase 3)**
- *Linear probe vs zero-shot vs MLP, when would you use each?* Zero-shot needs no labels (a good fallback for new attributes) but was 13–34 F1 points worse here. A linear probe is fast and well calibrated (ECE ~0.03–0.05 on the strong attributes) and nearly matches an MLP. The MLP adds 1–7 points where decision boundaries are non-linear (gender, material). All three reuse the same frozen embeddings, so no GPU is needed.
- *Why macro-F1 and not accuracy?* Classes are imbalanced (T-shirts 7k vs many types < 100). Accuracy rewards predicting the common classes; macro-F1 weighs every class equally. Article type: 90.5% accuracy but 81.4 macro-F1.
- *What is domain shift, and where did you see it?* Training (Myntra, Indian mass-market) and deployment (Farfetch, luxury) data differ. Symptoms: coats → "Handbags" (no coat class in training), 59% of luxury bags/shoes → "synthetic", 95% → "casual". Detected with independent signals (Farfetch titles), fixed with a category constraint, and by hiding untrustworthy attributes.
- *How did you validate predictions on data with no labels?* Independent weak signals: categories from product titles (89.6% agreement on 176k items) and colour words in titles (61% exact). Plus spot-checking disagreements, which is how the coat bug was found.
- *Why did adding a colour histogram not help the colour classifier?* Errors are adjacent shades (Grey vs Black, Blue vs Navy, Maroon vs Red). Colour-family accuracy is 78.5% vs 72.2% exact, so the limit is ambiguous labels, not missing colour information.
- *Why k-means over HDBSCAN?* HDBSCAN finds density-based clusters and labels sparse points as noise: good for discovery, but it gave 2 clusters for dresses and 35 for bags and left up to 32% unassigned. For a merchandising map every item needs a style at a comparable granularity. Silhouettes of 0.34–0.52 say styles form a continuum, which is honest to report.
- *What is hubness?* In high-dimensional embedding spaces some points (here, text prompts like "logo print") are close to almost everything. Naming by raw similarity picks the hubs; subtracting each word's average similarity (centring) surfaces what is distinctive.
- *Why not crop automatically?* Measured: auto-cropping the detector's most confident item drops street R@1 to 15.5 (from 30.4 uncropped), because outfit photos contain several items and the top box is often the wrong one. Letting the user pick lifts it to 37.3. **Measure before automating.**
- *Your blend scored better on the benchmark. Why is it off by default?* Three reasons: latency (+160 ms per query), memory (a second 1.2 GB model on a free CPU host), and behaviour. On real photos it favours colour and texture over shape, which isn't always what a shopper wants. A benchmark gain is necessary but not sufficient; product fit decides the default.

**Exact-match confidence (Phase 4)**
- *Why not just show similarity as a percentage ("98% match")?* Because it isn't one. On LookBench, results with similarity ≥ 0.95 were the exact product only 64% of the time. Showing "95%" would overpromise. I calibrated a probability instead and checked on held-out data that "90%" means right ~90% of the time.
- *What made the calibrator work?* The margin. An exact match stands out from the runner-up; a merely similar product sits in a crowd of similar items. Gap ≥ 0.05 → 89% exact. Using the gap to the 5th result as well guards against products with several photos in the catalog, where #1 and #2 are both correct.
- *What's ECE?* Expected calibration error: bin predictions by confidence and average |predicted − actual| weighted by bin size. 0.06 means predictions are off by about 6 points on average.
- *Your test photos all got "no exact match". Isn't that a failure?* It's the correct answer: the catalog is 2011–2019 and those products aren't in it. The failure mode worth preventing is the opposite, claiming an exact match that isn't. For a shopping app, under-claiming is the safe error.
- *How did you test the "screenshot from the internet" case?* Took 400 catalog photos, cropped, shrank, JPEG-compressed and brightened them, then searched. The original was #1 in 86%.
- *A user reported a wrong "skirt" label. How did you debug it?* Reproduced it on the test photos, found three causes (low-confidence boxes, garment parts inside a dress, product shots outside the detector's training domain), fixed each with a rule, re-checked all 11 photos, and added a unit test per rule. Then noticed the labels themselves were unreliable, so I removed them rather than patch them further.

**Named-model recognition (Phase 4)**
- *A user uploads a current best-seller and gets no exact match. Is the product broken?* The retrieval was right: all 10 results were small LV monogram pochettes, but the catalog (2019) simply doesn't stock a Félicie. I separated two questions: "is this exact listing in stock?" (needs data) and "which model is this?" (doesn't). The second is answered zero-shot from model names, and the app then shows our listings of that model or says it isn't stocked and links to a web search.
- *How can a model recognise a bag by name without examples?* Marqo-FashionSigLIP was trained on product images with titles, and luxury titles contain model names ("Neverfull MM", "Speedy 30"). So the text embedding of "Louis Vuitton Pochette Félicie" lands near photos of it. I verified rather than assumed: 92% top-1 on 2,892 catalog photos whose titles name one of 93 listed models, beating copying the model name from the nearest catalog photo (88% on the LV subset).
- *How do you avoid naming a model for everything?* A calibrator on [top score, gap to 2nd] trained with 6,000 negatives from brands not on the list. At P ≥ 0.8: 96% precision and a 0.3% false-alarm rate. Plus type gating, found from a real false alarm (pumps named as a bag).
- *Isn't evaluating on catalog titles circular?* Partly: the model may have seen similar Farfetch data in training, and titles are noisy labels. That's why I also checked out-of-catalog internet photos (the Félicie: 93%; 9 non-luxury photos: ≤ 6%) and degraded catalog photos (Neverfull, Speedy, Birkin: 14/15 named correctly). Weak spots are listed honestly: sibling models (Rockstud vs Roman Stud, Kelly vs Kelly Pochette) and Celine (3 test photos).
- *How would you extend it?* Add models to a YAML file, no retraining. For new launches the model may not know the name; then a few reference photos per model (few-shot prototypes) would be the next step.

**Explainability (Phase 4)**
- *How do you explain a visual search result?* Two layers. For shoppers: which attributes the photo and result share (type, colour, pattern...), with ≈ for neighbouring shades. For the model's actual reasoning: an occlusion heatmap showing which regions the similarity depends on. I'm explicit that attributes are a summary, not the model's reasons.
- *How do you know a saliency map is right?* Test it, don't eyeball it. Two checks on 150 LookBench street photos with garment boxes: pointing (hottest region inside the garment box: 67% vs 21% random) and deletion (hiding the top 25% cuts similarity by 0.070, hiding a random 25% by −0.010; top beats random 95% of the time). Deletion is the stronger test, because pointing has a strong centre-bias baseline (60%).
- *Why not attention maps or gradients, they're much faster?* I tried both. An exact decomposition of Marqo's attention pooling put the heat on background patches: its hottest patch was on the garment only 11% of the time, worse than random. That's the known "registers" effect in ViTs: some background patches store global information, so attention to them is real but says nothing about location. Gradient × input failed the deletion test. Occlusion costs ~50 forward passes (1.2 s) but is faithful by construction, so it's behind a toggle.
- *What is occlusion sensitivity?* Grey out one window of the image at a time, re-embed, and record how much the similarity drops; average over overlapping windows. Model-agnostic and needs no gradients.
